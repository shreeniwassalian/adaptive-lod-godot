# Speaker script (final deck)

## Slide 1

The project asks one narrow question: when the renderer is over its frame budget, can a controller coarsen vegetation detail automatically, in a bounded and stable way, and does that buy frame time? The scene is a Terrain3D forest with about 42 thousand vegetation instances and a very dense grass field. I compare the scene with no LOD at all against the adaptive version on the same scene and camera path. The screenshot is a real captured frame. In short: adaptive LOD cuts frame time by roughly three quarters against full detail; most of that comes from distance LOD itself, and the controller adds only a little on top.

## Slide 2

Rendering cost grows with triangles, draw calls and overdraw, and LOD trades detail for cost by switching to simpler representations with distance. The weakness of fixed distances is that they ignore the frame budget. The left side is general concept; the schematic is illustrative, not my meshes. The right side is what I built: distance LOD for trees, grass, bushes and debris, with thresholds scaled by one feedback-controlled bias in adaptive mode; the comparison is against None, where everything is drawn at full detail. Not implemented: per-instance importance, cross-fading, and mesh LOD for bushes.

## Slide 3

Two halves. Along the top is the adaptive part, which only runs in adaptive mode: the GPU render time of the main viewport is smoothed, compared with a target, and a PI controller with rate limits produces one global bias between 0 and 2, mapped to a per-category bias. Along the bottom is the shared pipeline, switched off entirely in None: for each tree, grass cell or chunk the policy compares camera distance in metres with thresholds, with hysteresis, and updates MultiMesh tiers under a change budget. With the bias at zero the thresholds are the starting distances, i.e. the adaptive system with the controller idle. The snippet is the real threshold code: baseline divided by one plus bias, clamped by a floor in metres. The constraint is expressed as distance floors and per-category caps, not literally as a two-level limit.

## Slide 4

Five contributions with statuses I can defend. One: thresholds driven by a controller instead of fixed; the recording shows the bias rising from zero to its maximum of 2.0 (the GPU was in a slow state that session) while the on-screen tree threshold moves. Two: everything in metres. Three: constrained coarsening, and here I am exact: it is distance floors for trees plus per-category caps, and the panel shows the LOD2 threshold stopping at 100 metres. That is not literally a two-level limit. Four: the benchmark harness, with the same path hash, interleaved runs and full CSV and JSON output. Five: the trade-off, where I have exact primitive counts and measured frame-time reductions against full detail but no perceptual metric. No claim of global novelty: the literature check on slide 8 was limited.

## Slide 5

All numbers come from the project benchmark harness: Godot 4.7.2 in a debug and editor build, because no export templates are installed, so a release build was not possible. Quadro P3200 laptop GPU, Forward+ on D3D12, VSync off, uncapped. A fixed 528 metre camera path at 8 metres per second, 10 seconds of warm-up and 60 seconds measured. Five runs per mode and resolution with the order rotated each repetition. Both modes see the same scene, seed and path hash; None simply has every LOD policy disabled. Fairness risks: earlier sessions showed the GPU changing speed by two to three times between runs, this series was stable; the build is debug; and the targets differ by profile. The screen recording is a demonstration, not benchmark data.

## Slide 6

Final series: 5 runs per mode and resolution, debug build, stable GPU state. At 1080p the average goes from 21.4 FPS with no LOD to 96.8 with adaptive LOD, 4.5x as many; mean frame time falls from 46.7 to 10.3 ms. At 1440p it goes from 19.6 to 72.1 FPS, 3.7x. The cause is mainly the amount of geometry: rendered primitives fall by about 90 percent and draw calls from about 3120 to 591. An honest reading: almost all of this comes from having distance LOD at all. Compared with the fixed-threshold version, which is not shown on this slide, the PI controller adds +0.2% at 1080p, where it stays idle because the GPU is under its target, and +2.4% at 1440p, where it saturates. So the controller is a safety mechanism that engages when the frame is over budget, not the main source of speed. 0 frames fell below 10 FPS in any of these runs. All numbers are from a debug and editor build.

At the same viewpoint, None draws all 403 thousand grass clumps and every tree at full detail; with adaptive on, the controller raises its bias to the maximum and the panel shows the grass drawn count falling to a few thousand and the tree LOD2 threshold moving from 150 to 100 metres, where its floor stops it. ## Slide 8

This slide places the result next to recent work from 2020 onwards. I want to be clear about its limits: I did a limited web search, not a systematic review, and very few papers report comparable numbers for LOD controllers on vegetation, so this is context rather than a ranking. The closest in spirit is a 2026 Visual Computer paper on frame-budget-driven adaptive LOD for VR, which reports the 95th percentile GPU frame time falling from 18.1 to 10.9 ms and over-budget frames falling from 84.5 to 7.0 percent against full-resolution rendering. Our equivalent, against no LOD, is a 99th percentile frame time falling from 72.0 to 12.9 ms at 1080p. The reductions are larger here, but the baselines, scenes and hardware differ, and ours is mostly distance LOD rather than the controller. The 2025 HPG work on GPU tree generation reports a median 7.74 ms full-scene frame on a Radeon RX 7900 XTX, which is a different goal, memory and generation, on much newer hardware. The 2024 CPU-usage LOD paper reports CPU time falling 15.1 to 14.2 ms and 10.8 to 10.3 ms, triangles down 15.8 and 25 percent, and FPS up from 66.1 to 90 and from 121 to 146. That is the closest comparison for our controller step: its gains are larger than our plus 0.2 and plus 2.4 percent, but its signal is CPU usage while ours is GPU time on a GPU-bound scene, and the scenes and hardware differ. It supports our future work item of a CPU-bound signal. The foliage path tracing paper shares the theme but I could not extract comparable numbers. Verify the figures against the papers before citing them.

## Slide 9

To conclude. What works: distance LOD for four vegetation categories with a PI-controlled bias, bounded by floors and caps, validated by four self-test suites run through a log-checking runner, and measured with a reproducible harness. What the data supports: against no LOD, the adaptive mode takes the scene from 21.4 to 96.8 FPS at 1080p and from 19.6 to 72.1 at 1440p, with about 90 percent fewer primitives. What it does not support: crediting the controller for that gain, since against fixed distances it adds only +0.2% and +2.4%, or claiming the 8 ms target is met at 1440p. Limits: debug build only, GPU state drift, no perceptual metric, and a limited literature search. The takeaway is a bounded, quality-preserving mechanism whose controller is a safety margin on top of ordinary distance LOD.
